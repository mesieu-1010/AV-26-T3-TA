### Part B - identify system
- Firstly, since the plant is a first-order system, we can model its velocity 
using the differential equation 'tau * dv/dt + v = K * u', where tau is the time 
constant and K is the plant gain. From the step_test plot, u_commanded is 
initially held at 15.0 deg/s. After the transient response has settled, the 
actual velocity becomes approximately constant, so dv/dt is ≈ 0. Therefore, 
the equation reduces to v = K * u. The actual steady-state velocity can be 
estimated from the slope of y_measured. Since y_measured is an angle, v = dy/dt.
From the plot, the measured angle increases by approximately 57 degrees over 
3 seconds, so v ≈ 57 / 3 ≈ 19 deg/s. Hence, K ≈ v / u ≈ 1.3.

- Secondly, For the time constant, tau represents how quickly the actuator 
approaches its new steady-state response after a sudden change in input.
For a first-order system, v(t) = v0 * e^(-t / tau) when the commanded velocity 
steps from 15 deg/s down to 0 deg/s. Before the step, the steady-state actual 
velocity is approximately v0 = 19 deg/s. After one time constant, the velocity 
should decay to approximately 36.8% of its initial value: v(tau) = 0.368 * v0. 
Therefore, v(tau) ≈ 0.368 * 19 ≈ 7.0 deg/s. From the step_test data, the measured 
velocity, estimated from the slope of y_measured, drops from approximately 19 deg/s 
to around 7 deg/s in about 0.07 to 0.08 seconds. Therefore, the time constant can 
be estimated as tau ≈ 0.075 s. This means the actuator responds relatively 
quickly to changes in commanded velocity, with the actual velocity moving 
significantly toward its new steady-state value within about 0.075 seconds.

- From the deadband_test plot, the nonlinearity is more consistent with backlash 
rather than a deadzone.

- In the plot named Deadband test - small velocity sweep near centre, u_commanded 
repeatedly switches between approximately +2 deg/s and -2 deg/s. At the beginning, 
y_measured increases to around 2.6 degrees, showing that a command of magnitude 
2 deg/s is capable of producing movement. However, after this initial movement, 
y_measured becomes almost flat even though u_commanded continues switching 
between positive and negative values.

- This behaviour is not consistent with a simple deadzone. If the magnitude of 
2 deg/s were completely inside a deadzone, the actuator should not have moved 
significantly at the beginning. Instead, the initial movement followed by a 
flat output during repeated reversals suggests that the internal actuator motion 
is moving through mechanical slack without immediately changing the measured 
output.

- This shape is characteristic of backlash. When the command reverses direction, 
the actuator must first move through the free-play region before the output 
angle begins moving in the opposite direction. Therefore, the Deadband test - 
small velocity sweep near centre plot provides evidence that the dominant 
nonlinearity is backlash rather than a deadzone.

- We estimate the backlash from the deadband_test using the motor-side travel 
during the slack period. With u_commanded ≈ 2 deg/s and K ≈ 1.3, the motor 
velocity is about v ≈ 1.3 * 2 ≈ 2.6 deg/s. Since the output stays nearly unchanged 
for about 1 second, the motor travels roughly 2.6 degrees through the slack. 
This is approximately one side of the backlash gap, so the full backlash is about 
BACKLASH ≈ 2 * 2.6 ≈ 5 degrees

- We split the backlash equally because the model assumes the slack is symmetric
around the output angle, giving approximately 2.6 degrees on each side. These 
values define the upper and lower slack boundaries. If the motor angle remains 
within this slack region, the measured output angle stays constant. Once the 
motor angle moves beyond either boundary, the measured output angle begins to 
move in the same direction as the motor.

### Part C - control design choices

- I implemented a PID controller using:

  u_commanded = kp * error + ki * integral + kd * derivative

  where:

  error = target - measured
  integral += error * dt
  derivative = (error - previousError) / dt

- I tuned the PID gains manually in stages.

- First, I set:

  kp = 1.0
  ki = 0.0
  kd = 0.0

- I then increased kp gradually:

  kp = 1.0
  kp = 1.5
  kp = 2.0
  kp = 2.5

  while keeping ki and kd equal to zero.

- The proportional gain mainly affects how strongly the controller reacts to the
  current error. Increasing kp makes the commanded velocity larger when the
  measured angle is far from the target, which reduces the rise time and makes the
  system reach the target faster. However, if kp is too large, the controller can
  become too aggressive and produce overshoot or oscillation. I therefore selected
  kp = 2.0 as a balance between a fast response and stable behaviour.

- Next, I kept kp fixed and increased kd gradually:

  kd = 0.02
  kd = 0.05
  kd = 0.08

- The derivative gain reacts to how quickly the error is changing. It provides
  damping as the measured angle approaches the target. This helps reduce overshoot
  and prevents the output from repeatedly oscillating around the target. Increasing
  kd too much can make the response unnecessarily slow or sensitive to small
  measurement changes, so I selected kd = 0.08 after comparing the settling
  behaviour of the different trials.

- Finally, I introduced the integral gain gradually:

  ki = 0.02
  ki = 0.05
  ki = 0.10
  ki = 0.15

- The integral term accumulates error over time. Its main purpose is to remove
  steady-state error that remains after the proportional and derivative terms have
  settled. This is particularly useful for the small on-centre correction, where
  backlash can cause the measured angle to remain slightly away from the target.
  I selected ki = 0.15 because it provided enough integral action to reduce the
  remaining steady-state error without causing significant overshoot or oscillation.

- The final PID gains used were:

  kp = 2.0
  ki = 0.15
  kd = 0.08

- These gains were chosen to balance the main closed-loop performance measures:

  - Rise time:
    kp was increased to make the controller respond more strongly to large errors,
    reducing the time required to approach the target.

  - Overshoot:
    kp was kept below the point where the response became too aggressive, and kd
    was added to slow the approach to the target when the error was changing
    quickly.

  - Oscillation:
    kd provides additional damping, while kp and ki were limited so that the
    controller does not repeatedly cross above and below the target.

  - Steady-state error:
    ki accumulates persistent error and continues applying a correction until the
    measured angle is close to the target.

- I also implemented anti-windup by limiting the accumulated integral value to:

  -20.0 <= integral <= 20.0

- Without this limit, a large error over a long period could cause the integral
  term to become very large. The stored integral could then continue pushing the
  actuator even after it approaches the target, increasing overshoot and settling
  time. Limiting the integral reduces this behaviour and helps the controller
  settle more cleanly.

- The final values were selected by repeatedly running Check 2 and comparing the
  final error, overshoot, settling time, and controller response plots for the
  large move, direction reversal, and small on-centre correction.
