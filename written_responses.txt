### Part A - recover the data (CAN decode)

Frame ID kept: 512 in decimal which is 0x200 in hexadecimal

Signal 1 (u_commanded): byte position / endianness / signed? / scale / offset
- Byte position: Bytes 3-4 (zero-based 2-3).
- Endianness: little endian
- Signed: 16-bit signed integer
- scale = 0.1 and offset = 0 


Signal 2 (y_measured): byte position / endianness / signed? / scale / offset
- Byte position: Bytes(1-2) (zero-based 0-1).
- Endianness: little endian
- Signed: 16-bit signed integer
- scale = 0.1 and offset = 0

### Part B - identify system
- Firstly, since the plant is a first-order system, we can model its velocity 
using the differential equation 'tau * dv/dt + v = K * u', where tau is the time 
constant and K is the plant gain. From the step_test plot, u_commanded is 
initially held at 15.0 deg/s. After the transient response has settled, the 
actual velocity becomes approximately constant, so dv/dt approx 0. Therefore, 
the equation reduces to v = K * u. The actual steady-state velocity can be 
estimated from the slope of y_measured. Since y_measured is an angle, v = dy/dt.
From the plot, the measured angle increases by approximately 57 degrees over 
3 seconds, so v ≈ 57 / 3 = 19 deg/s. Hence, K ≈ v / u ≈ 19 / 15 ≈ 1.27 ≈ 1.3.

- Secondly, For the time constant, tau represents how quickly the actuator 
approaches its new steady-state response after a sudden change in input.
For a first-order system: v(t) = v0 * e^(-t / tau) when the commanded velocity 
steps from 15 deg/s down to 0 deg/s. Before the step, the steady-state actual 
velocity is approximately: v0 ≈ 19 deg/s. After one time constant, the velocity 
should decay to approximately 36.8% of its initial value: v(tau) = 0.368 * v0. 
Therefore, v(tau) ≈ 0.368 * 19 ≈ 7.0 deg/s. From the step_test data, the measured 
velocity, estimated from the slope of y_measured, drops from approximately 19 deg/s 
to around 7 deg/s in about 0.07 to 0.08 seconds. Therefore, the time constant can 
be estimated as tau ≈ 0.075 s. This means the actuator responds relatively 
quickly to changes in commanded velocity, with the actual velocity moving 
significantly toward its new steady-state value within about 0.075 seconds.

- From the deadband_test plot, the nonlinearity is more consistent with backlash 
rather than a deadzone.

- In the plot named Deadband test - small velocity sweep near centre, u_commanded 
repeatedly switches between approximately +2 deg/s and -2 deg/s. At the beginning, 
y_measured increases to around 2.6 degrees, showing that a command of magnitude 
2 deg/s is capable of producing movement. However, after this initial movement, 
y_measured becomes almost flat even though u_commanded continues switching 
between positive and negative values.

- This behaviour is not consistent with a simple deadzone. If -/+2 deg/s were 
completely inside a deadzone, the actuator should not have moved significantly 
at the beginning. Instead, the initial movement followed by a flat output during 
repeated reversals suggests that the internal actuator motion is moving through 
mechanical slack without immediately changing the measured output.

- This shape is characteristic of backlash. When the command reverses direction, 
the actuator must first move through the free-play region before the output 
angle begins moving in the opposite direction. Therefore, the Deadband test - 
small velocity sweep near centre plot provides evidence that the dominant 
nonlinearity is backlash rather than a deadzone.

- We estimate the backlash from the deadband_test using the motor-side travel 
during the slack period. With u_commanded ≈ 2 deg/s and K ≈ 1.3, the motor velocity 
is about v ≈ 1.3 * 2 = 2.6 deg/s. Since the output stays nearly unchanged for 
about 1 second, the motor travels roughly 2.6 degrees through the slack. This is 
approximately one side of the backlash gap, so the full backlash is about 
BACKLASH ≈ 2 * 2.6 ≈ 5 degrees

- We split it equally because the model assumes the slack is symmetric around 
the output angle, giving about -/+2.6 degrees on each side.
